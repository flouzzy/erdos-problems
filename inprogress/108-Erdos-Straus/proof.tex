\section{Axiomatic Definitions and Context}
\begin{definition}
For any integer $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $n \ge 2$, the Erdős-Straus equation is defined as the Diophantine equation:
\[\frac{4}{n} = \frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{y} + \frac{1}{z}\]
where the variables $x, y, z \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ are strictly positive integers.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Contextual Literature Research}
The problem was formulated by Paul Erdős and Ernst G. Straus in 1948. Previous works on Diophantine equations of this type utilize local and global analytic principles. A notable approach is the use of modular reduction and polynomial parametrization to solve specific congruence classes. As demonstrated in the paper \textit{"A Complete Congruence System for the Erdos-Straus Conjecture"} (available on ArXiv), analyzing the structure of solutions through decomposition into sums of fractions with identically sized numerators yields significant insights into the conjecture.

\section{Proof Strategy and Lemma Isolation}
The selected approach is to partition the space of integers $n$ according to their congruence class modulo a highly composite integer, typically $4$ for base cases. We break the conjecture down into specific intermediate sub-problems.

\begin{lemma}
For $n = 4k+3$ where $k \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$, the Erdős-Straus equation admits a solution.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $n = 4k+3$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$.
We explicitly construct a solution by setting $x = k+1$, $y = n(k+1)+1$, and $z = n(k+1)(n(k+1)+1)$.
First, we substitute $x = k+1$ into the equation. We aim to show that:
\[ \frac{4}{n} - \frac{1}{k+1} = \frac{1}{y} + \frac{1}{z} \]
We evaluate the left-hand side with a common denominator $n(k+1)$:
\begin{align*}
\frac{4}{n} - \frac{1}{k+1} &= \frac{4(k+1) - n}{n(k+1)} \\
&= \frac{4k + 4 - (4k+3)}{n(k+1)} \\
&= \frac{1}{n(k+1)}
\end{align*}
Now, we express $\frac{1}{n(k+1)}$ as a sum of two unit fractions using the standard Egyptian fraction identity $\frac{1}{A} = \frac{1}{A+1} + \frac{1}{A(A+1)}$.
Setting $A = n(k+1)$, we obtain:
\begin{align*}
\frac{1}{n(k+1)} &= \frac{1}{n(k+1)+1} + \frac{1}{n(k+1)(n(k+1)+1)}
\end{align*}
By identifying the terms, we set:
\begin{align*}
y &= n(k+1)+1 \\
z &= n(k+1)(n(k+1)+1)
\end{align*}
Since $n \ge 3$ and $k \ge 0$, it is clear that $x > 0$, $y > 0$, and $z > 0$. Therefore, $(x, y, z) = (k+1, n(k+1)+1, n(k+1)(n(k+1)+1))$ is a valid solution in positive integers.
\end{proof}
